Part 4: Introduction to Personal Verb Initial Mutation

More Pronouns

We have created a lot of sentences using "I" ("ņra") as the subject. But what about using other pronouns? How do we say "we", "you", or "she" in Pwekish?

Let's look at the pronouns commonly used in Pwekish. We classify them into three categories: first person, second person, and third person.

Pronoun Pronunciation Meaning
First Person
ņra /ŋra/ I, me
ņris /ŋris/ we, us (including the listener)
ņos /ŋos/ we, us (excluding the listener)
Second Person
ntyu /n̩ˈtɕu/ you (singular)
ntyus /n̩ˈtɕus/ you (plural)
Third Person
nrow /nrow/ he, him; she, her; it
nrows /nrows/ they, them

Personal Verb Initial Mutation

Pwekish verbs conjugate based on the subject of the sentence. This means that the action word changes depending on who is performing the action.

  • For actions done by the first person (i.e., I and we), the verb remains unchanged.
  • For the second person and third person, the first letter of the verb changes according to a fixed set of rules.
  • This is known as Personal Verb Initial Mutation (PVIM).

PVIM is a vital concept in Pwekish. If you want to say something as simple as "he sees me", you need to know how the verb conjugates to make the sentence grammatically correct, much like how "go" changes to "goes" for third-person singular subjects in English.

How PVIM Works

The main mechanism of PVIM is adding special prefixes to a verb to alter its pronunciation. There are three prefixes used for PVIM: "-h", "h-", and "n-". They apply to the first letter of a word and change how that letter is pronounced.

Generally, these three prefixes function as follows:

  • "-h" is the voiceless marker, which turns the initial letter into a voiceless consonant.
  • "h-" is the voiced marker, which turns the initial letter into a voiced consonant.
  • "n-" is the nasal marker, which turns the initial letter into a nasal consonant.

Note: This specific acoustic change applies only to certain letters.

Example

Now, let's take a look at how the verbs we have already learned change under PVIM.

Do you see the pattern? Probably not yet! That is because we haven't introduced the Three-Letters Law, a powerful method for memorizing the conjugation rules of PVIM.

In short, the Three-Letters Law divides the letters of Pwekish into groups of three. PVIM can then be understood as shifting and choosing the right letter within that specific group. Because PVIM is a complex concept, we will break it down bit by bit in the next chapter.
