Part 2: Adjectives and Adverbs

Adjectives

Adjectives describe the attributes or characteristics of an object. In English, we use words like "small," "big," "beautiful," and "amazing." Pwekish features the exact same type of words, and they play a crucial role in forming detailed sentences.

There are two distinct types of adjectives in Pwekish: adjective roots and adjectivized words.

Adjective Roots

Adjective roots are words that are naturally adjectives on their own. They may or may not end with the letter "e". Below is a table showcasing a few common adjective roots.

Word Pronunciation Meaning
sil /sil/ big
rin /rin/ small
pres /pres/ many
masnusa /masˈnusa/ tired
dipnusa /dipˈnusa/ hungry
mlo /m̩ˈlo/ beautiful
ine /ˈine/ cool
fi /ɸi/ hot
peke /ˈpeke/ good
wallu /ˈwaʎu/ bad

Adjectivized Words

Adjectivized words are words derived from other parts of speech and transformed into adjectives using the adjectivizer suffix "-e" /e/ . Consequently, they always end with the letter "e". Below is a table showcasing a few common adjectivized words.

Word Pronunciation Meaning
adie /ˈadi̯e/ more
runie /ˈruni̯e/ few
ayae /ˈajɛ/ difficult
dəkbite /dəkˈbite/ special
esoke /eˈsoke/ fragrant; aromatic
orlee /ˈoɬeː/ clean
hwinke /ˈhʷinke/ happy; pleased
udae /ˈudɛ/ helpful
nue /ny/ female
yee /jeː/ male

Using Adjective Attributively

In Pwekish, we place the adjective after the noun it modifies. Additionally, when using an adjective root, we must attach the adjectivizer suffix "-e" to it.

For example, "big apple" translates to "bimbo sile". As you can see, the adjective root sil receives an -e at the end and is placed after the noun bimbo. Translating word-for-word, it reads as "apple big."

Even if an adjective root naturally ends in "e", we still add the "-e" suffix. For example, "cool water" is written as "xe inee". When this happens the "ee" is pronounced as a long vowel.

Adding the "-e" suffix will not alter the stress pattern. That means if a word has a stress on the first syllable, even adding the "-e" may increase the number of syllables, the stress will remain on the first syllable.

We do not add "-e" to adjectivized words, since they already have one. For example, "helpful person" translates to "nur udae" (not "nur udaee").

Example Sentences

Sentence Meaning
Ņra yenmor sile nuke. I like big cars.
Ntyu gale fie ndip. You eat hot meat.
Nrow sukdi ayae yhe. He does difficult homework.

The structures above show adjectives used in their attributive form. Adjectives in Pwekish can also be used in a predicative form, as shown in the next section.

Predicative Form

Adjectives in Pwekish can function directly as verbs meaning "to be adjective". When used this way, they undergo standard PVIM based on the subject. Crucially, when an adjective functions as a verb, we do not add the adjectivizer "-e". For example, "He is helpful." translates directly to "Nrow hhudae.".

Fun Fact:

Of course, you could technically use the copula "pe" (to be) and form a sentence like "Nrow nur udae npe" (He is a helpful person). However, Pwekish favours simpler sentences. The copula "pe" is generally reserved for linking two distinct nouns together ("X is Y"). For describing traits, speakers almost always use the predicative verb form.

Adjective Chains

When multiple adjectives modify a single noun, we chain them.

  • For the attributive form, they are chained in the opposite direction of English.
  • For the predicative form, they are chained in the same direction as English.

For example, the sentence "Many good foods are hot and fragrant." translates to "Pdipn peke prese nfi hhesoke.".

Example Sentences

Sentence Meaning
Ņra masnusa. I am tired.
Ntyu ndəkbite. You are special.
Tyep rine hmlo. The small bird is beautiful.
Xe orlee npeke. Clean water is good.
Ņra tyep sile dipnusae runie sano. I have a few hungry big birds.
Nrows npeke hhudae. They are good and helpful.
Teibəl mloe rine prese npeke dhəkbite. Many small and beautiful tables are good and special.

Possessive Adjectives and Pronouns

In English, possessive adjectives (my, your, its) describe the owner of a noun, while possessive pronouns (mine, yours, theirs) replace a noun phrase entirely.

In Pwekish, possessive adjectives and possessive pronouns are grammatically identical. Both are formed by simply adding the adjectivizer "-e" to a personal pronoun. The only difference is structural: when used attributively (after a noun), they function as possessive adjectives; when used predicatively (as a verb), they function as possessive pronouns.

The table below summarizes the possessive pronouns in Pwekish.

Word Pronunciation Meaning
First Person
ņrae /ŋrɛ/ my; mine
ņrise /ˈŋrise/ our; ours (including the listener)
ņose /ˈŋose/ our; ours (excluding the listener)
Second Person
ntyue /n̩ˈtɕy/ your; yours (singular)
ntyuse /n̩ˈtɕuse/ your; yours (plural)
Third Person
nrowe /ˈnrowe/ his, her, its; his, hers, its
nrowse /ˈnrowse/ their; theirs

Example Sentences

Sentence Meaning
Yenmor ņrae nsil. My car is big.
Maw nrowe dhipnusa. Her cat is hungry.
Awú nrowse nrow h'wipteptep. Their dog chases him.
Vurún rine runie pdipn ņrae dhip. A few small mice eat my food.
Dorn hņrise. The house is ours.
Nrow hņrae. It's mine.
Bimbo prese hnrowse. Many apples are theirs.
Sewu sile ņrae hntyue. My big fish is yours.

Notice: Possessive adjectives must be placed after all other adjectives in a noun phrase (e.g., "sewu sile ņrae" -> "fish big my").

Adverbs of Manner

This section focuses strictly on adverbs that describe the manner or way an action is performed.

In Pwekish, these adverbs behave exactly like attributive adjectives, except they are placed immediately after a verb instead of a noun. In fact, many adjectives we have already learned can double as adverbs without any modification.

For example, "The cat hungrily eats the fish." -> "Maw sewu dhip dipnusae.". In this sentence, dipnusae means "hungrily", and it is positioned directly after the mutated verb dhip (eats).
