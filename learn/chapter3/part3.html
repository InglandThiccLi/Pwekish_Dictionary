Part 3: Gerunds and Nouns

Gerunds

Gerunds are words that are formed from a verb but function exactly like a noun. In English, this conversion is done by adding "-ing" to a verb (e.g., "eat" becomes "eating"). In Pwekish, the conversion is just as simple: we attach the suffix "-n" /n/ to the root verb.

Here are a few examples:

dip /dip/ to eat -> dipn /ˈdipn̩/ eating

mor /mor/ to go -> morn /morn/ going

koko /ˈkoko/ to play -> kokon /ˈkokon/ playing

Beyond verbs, Pwekish also allows you to convert adjectives into gerunds by adding the exact same "-n" suffix. In these cases, the gerund translates to "being adjective":

sil /sil/ big -> siln /ˈsiln̩/ being big

dipnusa /dipˈnusa/ hungry -> dipnusan /dipˈnusan/ being hungry

hwinke /ˈhʷinke/ happy -> hwinken /ˈhʷinken/ being happy

Using Gerunds

Because a gerund is treated as a noun in Pwekish, it can easily function as either the subject or the object of a sentence.

Example Sentences

Sentence Meaning
Ņra teptepn nuke. I like running.
Ntyu xepten mhor. You go swimming.
Baln npeke. Reading is good.
Dipnusan hwallu. Being hungry is bad.
Nrow hwinken hnusa. He wants to be happy.

A sentence like "Ņra bimbo dipn nuke" (I like eating apples) is perfectly valid in Pwekish. While grammatically correct, a more natural and common way for native speakers to express this is "Ņra bimbo dipnuke" (I like to eat apples). This structures uses a process called verb stacking, which we will explore in detail in the next chapter.

Nominalization

The suffix "-n" wears two hats. Not only does it create gerunds, but it also functions as a nominalizer, a tool that converts a root word into a noun with a meaning closely related to the original word.

For example, the noun "baln" (book) is formed by adding "-n" to the verb "bal" (to read). However, as we established above, "baln" also literally means the gerund "reading".

Since the gerund and the derived noun are spelled and pronounced identically, you must rely entirely on context to interpret the speaker's true meaning. Consider these two examples:

1. Ņra baln sano.
✅ I have a book.
❌ I have reading.

2. Ņra baln nuke.
✅ I like the book.
✅ I like reading.

In instances where a single standalone sentence is ambiguous, you will simply need to look at the broader conversation to determine the exact meaning.

Nominalization is incredibly flexible. The nouns derived from this process can carry broad, nuanced, or multiple meanings, and there is no strict linguistic formula to predict the exact noun a root will create. When in doubt, it is always best to double-check the dictionary!
