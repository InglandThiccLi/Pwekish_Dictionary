Part 1: The Three-Letters Law

A Simple Example

Let's talk about how the Three-Letters Law works. We will use the letter group "p, b, m" for this demonstration.

For words starting with p, let's take "pe" /pe/ (to be) as an example.

  • If the subject is first person (I and we), as we mentioned before, no conjugation is applied, so it remains "pe" /pe/ .
  • If the subject is second person (you), it becomes "hpe", pronounced as /be/ .
  • If the subject is third person (he, she, it, they), it becomes "npe", pronounced as /me/ .

Look carefully at the pronunciation of these words. Have you noticed the pattern of how the consonant changes?

/pe/ -> /be/ -> /me/

That's right! We can see that these sounds all belong to the letter group "p, b, m". The progression is:

p (1st person) -> b (2nd person) -> m (3rd person)

This conjugation rule applies to all words starting with the letter "p".

Adding the Pronunciation Alteration Prefixes

Once we know this shifting pattern, we can easily predict the pronunciation of words that undergo PVIM. To spell it out, we just add the prefixes "-h", "h-", or "n-" to the first letter of the word to change its pronunciation.

For words starting with the letter "p", we only need the voiced marker "h-" and the nasal marker "n-", as we want to alter the pronunciation to /b/ for the second person mutation and /m/ for the third person mutation, respectively.

You might wonder: Instead of writing "pe", "hpe", "npe", why don't we just write "pe", "be", "me"?

The reason is that we want to preserve the original root word even after applying PVIM. This prevents confusion while reading, as an unaltered word might otherwise look identical to a mutated word.

Spelling Note: The prefixes "h-" and "n-" are never capitalized. For example, if a word needs to be capitalized, we write "hPe" instead of "Hpe".

More Examples

What about words starting with "b" and "m"? Since they are in the same "p, b, m" letter group, their conjugation follows a similar pattern. Let's look at "bay" (to see) and "mor" (to go).

1st Person 2nd Person 3rd Person
to see
bay /baj/ nbay /maj/ bhay /paj/
to go
mor /mor/ mhor /por/ hmor /bor/

The changes to the first consonant flow like this:

bay: /b/ -> /m/ -> /p/

mor: /m/ -> /p/ -> /b/

It is simply a rotational shift within the "p, b, m" group:

  • For 1st person mutation, the shift amount is 0, so the letter remains the same.
  • For 2nd person mutation, the shift amount is 1. Therefore, "p" shifts to "b", "b" shifts to "m", and "m" wraps back around to "p".
  • For 3rd person mutation, the shift amount is 2. Therefore, "p" shifts to "m", "b" wraps back around to "p", and "m" wraps back around to "b".

Summary

The entire process of PVIM can be broken down into these steps:

Step Explanation Example
1. Identify the Letter Group Look at the first letter of the verb to determine its group. "bay" is in the "p, b, m" group.
2. Determine the Shift Amount Find the shift amount based on the grammatical person. Third person mutation requires a shift amount of 2.
3. Rotate the Sound Shift the first letter rotationally within its group to find the target pronunciation. Shifting "b" twice gives us "b" -> "m" -> "p", so the target pronunciation starts with /p/.
4. Apply the Correct Prefix Attach the appropriate pronunciation alteration marker to the root letter based on the target sound. Since /p/ is a voiceless consonant, we use the voiceless marker "-h", resulting in "bhay".

Now, you should be able to say "He sees me!" in Pwekish: "Nrow ņra bhay!".

Example Sentences

Sentence Meaning
Ņtyu awú nbay. You see a dog.
Ntyus dorn mhor. You guys go home.
Nrow baln bhal. He reads a book.
Awú sewu bhay. The dog sees a fish.
