Part 4: PVIM for Compound Letters

Dealing with "y"-Ending Compound Letters

When handling words that start with a compound letter ending in "y", the process is straightforward: we only look at the first letter and match it to its corresponding letter group when performing PVIM. We follow this exact same rule for consonant clusters (such as "pl", "lw", and "kr") that are not explicitly on the official compound letter list.

Let's take "ty" as an example.

  1. We isolate the first letter, "t".
  2. "t" belongs to the "t, d, n" group, which yields the base mutations "ty", "dy", and "ny" for the first, second, and third person.
  3. After applying the standard pronunciation alteration markers to the initial letter, they are spelled "ty", "hty", and "nty".

Determining the exact pronunciation can be slightly tricky. You need to refer to the unmutated base forms ("ty", "dy", and "ny") to figure out the sound. In this example, "ty", "dy", and "ny" are pronounced /tɕ/, /dʑ/, and /ɲ/. While some of these intermediate compound letters (like "ny") cannot be found in the dictionary, most are intuitive and straightforward. You simply pronounce them exactly as they are spelled.

Below is a table showing the PVIM of compound letters ending with "y". The intermediate base forms used to determine pronunciation are shown in brackets.

1st person mutation 2nd person mutation 3rd person mutation
ty (ty) /tɕ/ hty (dy) /dʑ/ nty (ny) /ɲ/
dy (dy) /dʑ/ ndy (ny) /ɲ/ dhy (ty) /tɕ/
sy (sy) /ɕ/ hsy (zy) /ʑ/ nsy (ry) /rʲ/
zy (zy) /ʑ/ nzy (ry) /rʲ/ zhy (sy) /ɕ/
hy (hy) /ç/ hhy (xy) /ʁʲ/ nhy ('y) /ʔj/

Exceptions

There are two major exceptions where you cannot deduce the pronunciation directly from the spelling: the PVIM of "gy" and "ņy". These require rote memorization, and their mutations flow as follows:

1st person mutation 2nd person mutation 3rd person mutation
gy (gy) /z/ ngy (ņy) /ŋʲ/ ghy (ky) /s/
ņy (ņy) /ŋʲ/ ņhy (ky) /s/ hņy (gy) /z/
Fun Fact:

You might notice that "ky" and "gy" make a surprising /s/ and /z/ sound, respectively. This is due to a historical sound shift that occurred when Pwekish evolved from Middle Pwekish into Modern Pwekish. Furthermore, both the letter "c" and the compound letter "cy" are used to represent the historical "ky" sound in modern Pwekish orthography.


Compound Letters with Irregular PVIM

There are five additional compound letters that possess highly irregular PVIM patterns. Their pronunciations must be memorized individually.

1st person mutation 2nd person mutation 3rd person mutation
ts (ts) /ts/ hts (dz) /dz/ nts (nr) /nr/
dz (dz) /dz/ ndz (nr) /nr/ dhz (ts) /ts/
si (si) /si/ or /ɕ(ə)/ hsi (zi) /zi/ or /ʑ(ə)/ nsi (ri) /ri/
yu (yu) /ju/ or /ɥ(u)/ nyu (ñu) /ɲu/ or /ɲɥ(u)/ yhu (lu) /lu/ or /lu̯/
rl (rl) /ɬ/ rhl (sl) /sl/ hrl (zy) /ʑ/

Note: For "yu", "nyu", and "yhu", a secondary pronunciation is triggered whenever they are immediately followed by a vowel. We will cover the specific pronunciation shifts of "si" in a later chapter.

The Rest Are Pretty Easy

With the most difficult patterns out of the way, we are only missing a few final compound letters. Fortunately, they are incredibly easy to manage.

  • Vowel Compounds: The vowel letter group rules apply to all vowels, including those spelled as compound letters. For example, the PVIM for "ae" is simply "ae", "hae", and "hhae".
  • The "cy" Compound: The PVIM for "cy" behaves exactly like the single letter "c".
  • The "ll" Compound: The PVIM for "ll" is almost identical to "l", with the only difference in the pronunciation of "ll" and "l".
1st person mutation 2nd person mutation 3rd person mutation
c /s/ hc /z/ nc /ŋʲ/
cy /s/ hcy /z/ ncy /ŋʲ/
l /l/ hl /y/ nl /ɲ/
ll /ʎ/ hll /y/ nll /ɲ/

What's Next?

And that concludes everything you need to know about PVIM! It is a challenging but vital core concept of the Pwekish language. We will take a well-deserved break from heavy grammar in the next chapter to explore adjectives, adverbs, and gerunds, which are much easier to learn and manage!
